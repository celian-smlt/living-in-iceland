Three numbers to picture Iceland. 394,324 people: that’s the whole country, smaller than many single cities. Two out of three of them live around the capital, Reykjavík. Almost 100 percent of the electricity comes from renewables: hydropower and geothermal plants like this one, where steam from deep under the ground drives the turbines. And about one resident in five was born abroad, so foreign workers are a normal part of the economy.

ÍSLAND · Célian Semelet04
Geographically, Iceland is about one fifth of the size of France, in the North Atlantic, just below the Arctic Circle. The fascinating part is underground. The island sits right on the Mid-Atlantic Ridge: the western half is on the North American plate, the eastern half on the Eurasian plate, and they drift apart by about two centimetres a year. That glowing rift in the middle is why there are around thirty active volcanic systems, and since 2021 several eruptions near the capital. Add glaciers on about eleven percent of the land, and you understand the nickname: the land of fire and ice.

ÍSLAND · Célian Semelet05
A very quick history. Vikings from Norway, on ships like this one, settled the island in 874, and in 930 they created the Althing, one of the oldest parliaments in the world. Iceland then spent centuries under Norwegian and Danish rule, until it became a republic on 17 June 1944. In 1994 it joined the European Economic Area, but not the EU. That detail matters for us: as French citizens, we can work there freely. 2008 was a shock, with the collapse of the banks, but tourism brought the economy back. And since 2021, volcanoes have been erupting again near the capital.

Now the money. Surprise number one: Iceland has no legal minimum wage. Instead, unions and employers negotiate collective agreements, and by law those minimums apply to every worker, Icelandic or not. Look at these columns on a black-sand beach: the ice one is the French SMIC, about 1,800 euros. The lowest Icelandic wage is about 476,000 krónur, roughly 3,500 euros gross: almost twice as tall. The median full-time salary is 826,000 krónur, about 6,000 euros, and the average is 913,000, about 6,700 euros. After tax and pension, the median worker takes home around 4,400 euros. Sounds amazing, right? Hold that thought until the next slide.

Here’s the catch. Welcome to downtown Reykjavík: colourful tin houses and the Hallgrímskirkja church. A 50 square metre flat here costs about 4,700 krónur per square metre, so around 235,000 krónur a month, roughly 1,700 euros. I calculated it from the official rental registry, which gives about 4,500 per square metre in the city centre, plus the six percent increase of the last year, and it matches the average advertised rent in September 2026. That’s about forty percent of a median take-home pay. The only good news: heating is geothermal, so energy bills are very low.

Getting around. First fun fact: Iceland has no trains, none. In Reykjavík you take the yellow Strætó buses, like this one: 690 krónur a ride, about 5 euros, and the system stops charging you after three rides a day. A monthly pass is 12,000 krónur, about 88 euros. If you drive, petrol is around 200 krónur a litre, and since January 2026 there is something quite unique: every car, petrol or electric, pays a road fee of about 7 krónur for each kilometre driven.

Sources: US Trade.gov Country Commercial Guide, businessculture.org09
How do Icelanders do business? In one sentence: flat, direct and on time. Everybody uses first names, even with the CEO or the president, because most Icelanders don’t have family names: Jónsson simply means son of Jón. Be punctual, and if you’re late, call. Say what you mean and never promise what you can’t deliver. The hierarchy is flat: you can talk to the boss directly. Meetings are relaxed, often over coffee, but you’re expected to be prepared. And stay humble: in such an equal society, showing off is a real faux pas. By the way, the steaming pool behind is a heitur pottur, a geothermal hot tub, where Icelanders love to chat after work. For an American, the first names would feel very familiar — the modesty, maybe a little less!

So, how do you actually find a job? Start with the public employment service, the Directorate of Labour, which also runs EURES, the European job network: those ads are in English. Then Alfreð, the biggest job app in Iceland, Starfatorg for public-sector jobs, and sites like Störf, Job.is or Tvinna for tech. Who is hiring? Tourism, fishing — like this trawler in Reykjavík harbour — energy and the aluminium smelters that use it, tech and gaming, healthcare and construction. Registered unemployment was only 3.9 percent in August. And as EU citizens, we need no permit: you just register after three months and get your kennitala, the Icelandic ID number you need for everything.

07 · Niðurstaða — The verdict

Should you pack your bags?

Fire: go for it

  • Very high wages, strong unions
  • Open to EU workers, low unemployment
  • Safe, green, stunning nature
  • English widely spoken

Ice: think twice

  • Rent eats ≈ 40% of take-home pay
  • High taxes and prices
  • Dark winters, harsh weather
  • Icelandic needed for many jobs

Iceland pays well — if you can handle the price tag and the winter.

ÍSLAND · Célian Semelet11
So, should you pack your bags? It’s literally fire and ice. On the fire side: excellent wages protected by strong unions, an open door for EU workers, low unemployment, a safe and green country, and nearly everyone speaks English. On the ice side: rent takes around forty percent of your take-home pay, taxes and prices are high, winters are long and dark, and for many jobs you’ll need some Icelandic. My bottom line: Iceland pays well, if you can handle the price tag and the winter.

Takk fyrir: that’s “thank you” in Icelandic. And to end on fire and ice one last time: the northern lights over a glacier lagoon. I’m happy to take your questions now. Possible questions to prepare: Can you live there with only English? (Day to day yes, but many jobs ask for Icelandic.) Is Icelandic hard to learn? (Yes, it’s very close to Old Norse, but most people speak English.) How dark is it in winter? (About four to five hours of daylight in December in Reykjavík.)
